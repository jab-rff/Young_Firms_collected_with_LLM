COMBINED PIPELINE DOCUMENTATION

Introduction

This document presents a coherent description of the research pipeline used in the project to identify two related types of firms. The first type consists of firms founded in Denmark that later moved their own executive headquarters or main operations abroad. The second type consists of firms founded abroad by Danish founders. These two categories are closely related in practice because they often appear in similar sources and are frequently described using similar language. At the same time, they are analytically distinct and should not be conflated. A Danish-founded firm that later relocates abroad is not the same thing as a firm founded abroad by Danish entrepreneurs, and one of the central aims of the pipeline is precisely to separate these cases carefully.

The project uses three identification methods. The first is an article-based pipeline built around a large corpus of material from Borsen. The second is an LLM prompting pipeline that starts from a validated seed list and uses recall-oriented web discovery to find additional firms. The third is a Diffbot-based pipeline that starts from an externally assembled candidate list and then passes those firms through the same downstream validation logic. Although the three methods differ in their point of entry, they share an overall methodological philosophy. Early stages are designed to preserve plausible cases and maintain high recall. Later stages become stricter, more source-grounded, and more conservative. Final acceptance is always deferred to human close reading.

This structure reflects the difficulty of the underlying research problem. Headquarters relocations are often not stated directly. Founding histories can be incomplete, post hoc, or strategically simplified. Firms change names, move across legal entities, create holding structures, open foreign offices without relocating headquarters, or become acquired in ways that blur whether a foreign location reflects true relocation, ordinary expansion, or organizational integration into another company. As a result, the project cannot rely on a single source or a single step of automated classification. Instead, it uses a staged process in which discovery, filtering, evidence reconciliation, conservative validation, and human review are kept analytically distinct.


CHAPTER 1. BORSEN ARTICLE PIPELINE

The Borsen pipeline is the article-led arm of the project. Its purpose is to identify possible headquarters relocations of Danish firms from a large body of Danish business newspaper reporting. The central challenge in this setting is that relocation events are rarely described in explicit and standardized language. Business articles often speak in indirect terms, referring to international growth, new offices, foreign leadership, group restructuring, investment rounds, acquisitions, or a firm being “now based” somewhere else, without clearly stating whether the firm actually moved its own executive headquarters or core operations abroad. Because of this, the pipeline was designed as a multi-stage reduction process in which the article corpus is progressively narrowed while trying to retain genuinely plausible relocation cases.

The first stage consists of article collection. According to the existing project documentation, the material was collected from Borsen.dk through a search-based retrieval strategy. Since the website structure did not allow straightforward browsing of the full archive, the collection process relied on a broad set of search queries covering years, countries, regions, industries, and business-related keywords. By iterating across these queries, sorting options, and result pages, the project assembled a very large corpus of business reporting. The existing documentation describes the resulting dataset as containing approximately 816,399 unique articles. This stage was motivated by the need to maximize coverage at the outset, since later filtering would inevitably discard large amounts of irrelevant or weakly relevant material.

The second stage consists of structured information extraction from article text. Simpler approaches, such as searching article titles for relocation-related words or looking only for explicit mentions of moving, proved too weak because true relocation cases are often described only indirectly. The pipeline therefore moved toward firm-level extraction from the full article text. In the documented process, all firms mentioned in an article were identified, and a structured extraction model was used to derive information about each firm from the surrounding text. This included whether a firm was portrayed as Danish, whether it was associated with internationalization, whether merger and acquisition context was present, and whether the text suggested cross-country movement.

The logic of this stage is important. The purpose is not yet to decide whether a relocation occurred. Instead, the goal is to convert unstructured narrative reporting into a more systematic firm-level representation that can later be filtered and compared across cases. This matters because newspaper language is highly variable. One article may describe a firm as Danish, another may describe it as international, and another may focus on a foreign office or foreign management team. A structured extraction stage makes it possible to retain these signals separately rather than collapsing them too early into one broad judgment.

The Borsen documentation describes three successive extraction schemas. The first schema focuses on broad contextual firm characteristics, such as Danish origin, Danish presence, internationalization, investment, and merger or acquisition activity. The second schema focuses more directly on movement across countries by extracting whether a firm has moved, will move, or might move, together with possible origin and destination countries. The third schema refines this further by distinguishing the movement of the firm as a whole from the movement of only individuals, departments, production, or sales activity. This refinement is necessary because many false positives arise precisely when a foreign expansion or organizational reshuffling is mistaken for a headquarters relocation.

After extraction, the article-based pipeline applies several filters. The early filters are relatively inclusive. They retain cases where Danish origin or Danish presence co-occurs with signals that the firm may no longer be based in Denmark or may have developed a meaningful foreign center of activity. Later filters become more restrictive by focusing more directly on cross-country movement and by excluding cases where only people, departments, or production appear to move. According to the existing documentation, the resulting candidate set falls substantially across these stages, from roughly 60,000 cases after the broad first filter to around 10,000 after stronger movement filtering, then to around 8,700 after distinguishing more carefully between headquarters-related movement and other forms of international activity, and finally to around 6,000 after restricting attention to relocations from Denmark or cases with unclear origin or destination.

Once the sample has been reduced through extraction and filtering, the pipeline moves into a classification stage. At this point the project no longer asks merely whether an article contains relocation-relevant signals, but whether a given firm-article pair plausibly represents a headquarters or main-operations relocation from Denmark to another country. In the documented setup, GPT-4o was used to assign structured scores for relocation, Danishness, and merger or acquisition context. This stage is explicitly designed to work with indirect evidence. That is a crucial methodological choice, because many true cases would otherwise be missed if only explicit relocation wording were counted. At the same time, the model is instructed not to treat ordinary foreign office openings as sufficient evidence.

The classification stage matters because articles can mention many firms and can contain multiple overlapping narratives. A single article may discuss an acquisition, a foreign expansion, and the background of several founders all at once. The model-based scoring step allows the project to focus attention on the firm-article pairs most likely to be relevant. According to the documentation, pairs with relocation scores above 0.7 were retained, which reduced the set to 1,279 firms across roughly 2,100 articles. This substantial reduction shows why a staged pipeline is necessary: the article corpus is too broad to interpret manually from the start, but too noisy to trust after a single pass of filtering.

After classification, the Borsen pipeline moves to data triangulation. The project documentation describes this as the point at which the information contained in the articles is considered largely exhausted, and external sources become necessary. Three sources are brought together here: destination-country signals extracted from the article, GPT-based inference about the firm’s current headquarters location, and Diffbot as an independent structured web source. The purpose is not merely to collect more information, but to cross-check whether different evidence channels converge on a similar interpretation. If article-based signals, model-based inference, and structured web data all point in the same direction, the case becomes more credible. If they disagree, the case becomes a candidate for caution or deeper review.

This triangulation stage is particularly important because some of the most persistent false positives arise from merger and acquisition contexts. A firm may appear to have a foreign headquarters simply because it has been acquired by a foreign company, or because a later source describes the parent company rather than the original operating firm. The Borsen documentation notes this problem explicitly and describes matching article-based merger and acquisition signals to the Orbis M&A database. The aim is not to exclude all acquired firms automatically, since some acquisitions may coincide with meaningful continuity of the operating company abroad. Rather, the aim is to preserve this context so that later interpretation can distinguish between independent relocation and acquisition-linked organizational change.

The final stage of the Borsen pipeline is manual close reading. At this point the system has reduced the initial article universe to a much smaller set of candidate firms, each supported by extracted signals, model-based classification, and triangulated external evidence. Human review then examines the original article together with additional web sources, LinkedIn, the Danish business register, ORBIS, and related sources. The purpose of this stage is to make the final substantive distinction between firms founded in Denmark that later relocated abroad, entrepreneurs who moved abroad and then founded firms there, and false positives that do not fit either category.

The documentation emphasizes that some cases remain ambiguous even after extensive evidence gathering. For that reason, the project uses conservative coding rules. If Danes appear to have founded a firm but the initial location cannot be determined with confidence, the case is coded as founded abroad rather than as founded in Denmark. Likewise, if the present headquarters location cannot be identified confidently, the case is removed from the target set rather than included on weak grounds. These rules are methodologically important because they show that the pipeline is designed not to maximize the number of accepted firms, but to preserve analytical clarity in the final dataset.

Taken as a whole, the Borsen method is best understood as a high-recall article-mining pipeline that gradually shifts from broad collection to structured extraction, then to model-based classification, external triangulation, and close reading. Its main strength is that it can recover relocation signals that appear in business reporting even when those signals are indirect or narrative rather than standardized. Its main weakness is that business reporting is noisy and often blends together relocation, foreign expansion, acquisition, founder migration, and legal restructuring. The later stages of the pipeline exist precisely to counter that weakness.


CHAPTER 2. LLM PROMPTING AND DIFFBOT PIPELINES

The LLM prompting pipeline and the Diffbot pipeline are documented together because, despite their different entry sources, they share the same downstream logic. Both are designed to identify the same two substantive categories: firms founded in Denmark that later moved their own executive headquarters or main operations abroad, and firms founded abroad by Danish founders. Both are also built around the same core principle: candidate generation should remain broad enough to preserve plausible cases, but final inclusion should depend on stricter evidence reconciliation and conservative validation.

This shared design responds to a general problem in firm-level web research. Information about founding location, founder identity, headquarters relocation, and current headquarters status is often fragmented across multiple sources that differ in reliability and specificity. Company websites may emphasize branding over legal history. News coverage may describe foreign expansion without clarifying whether executive functions moved. Startup databases may compress complex organizational histories into a few fields. Founder biographies may reveal Danish origin without clarifying where the company was initially established. Because of this, a pipeline that treats the first retrieved source as truth is likely to produce unstable and conceptually mixed results. The staged design used here attempts to solve that problem by separating discovery from evaluation.

The project works with two origin tracks. The first track concerns firms founded in Denmark that later moved their own executive headquarters, main office, or main operations abroad. The second concerns firms founded abroad by Danish founders. These tracks are deliberately kept separate because they answer different research questions. A Danish founder abroad is not the same as a Denmark-founded firm, a foreign office is not enough to establish relocation, acquisition-only cases are not enough to establish independent movement of the focal firm, and legal redomiciling is not automatically evidence of executive headquarters relocation. The entire pipeline is built to preserve these distinctions, because raw web material tends to blur them.

The LLM prompting pipeline begins from a validated seed list of already known firms. This list serves both as an exclusion device and as an anchor for discovery. It prevents the search process from repeatedly surfacing the same well-known cases and also provides concrete examples of the type of firms the system is supposed to find. The discovery stage is recall-oriented. Rather than attempting immediate validation, it searches broadly for additional firms that could plausibly fit one of the target categories. In the current implementation, this is done through bounded discovery buckets such as industries, destination countries, city-pair logics, and selected combinations of sector and destination. This bucketed structure is useful because it reduces the tendency of open-ended search to keep returning only a small number of famous firms. It also increases the chance of finding obscure or long-tail candidates that a narrower search strategy would miss.

The output of this stage is a set of structured candidate leads. Each lead records the firm name, the requested origin track, short evidence notes, source URLs, and tentative hints about founding location, founding year, possible abroad location, and the kind of signal that triggered retrieval. These leads are intentionally weak in the sense that they do not claim to constitute verified cases. Their purpose is to preserve provenance and to capture why a firm was surfaced. This design is methodologically important because it allows the pipeline to remain useful even when the early evidence is indirect, partial, or uncertain. Discovery should not fail simply because the first retrieved source is incomplete.

Because the same firm can appear multiple times across different search buckets, the next stage deduplicates the candidate set. This stage merges repeated discoveries by normalized firm name and preserves the distinct source URLs and discovery contexts attached to them. The reason for doing this is not only practical. It also prevents later stages from mistaking repeated mentions of the same firm for independent corroboration. Frequency of retrieval is not the same as quality of evidence, and the deduplication stage helps maintain that distinction.

After deduplication, the LLM prompting pipeline enters a first structured candidate extraction stage. This stage remains recall-first, but it is no longer pure discovery. The task now is to ask whether a candidate could plausibly fit the relevant research track. For the Denmark-founded track, the question is whether the firm could plausibly have been founded in Denmark and later moved its own headquarters, main office, or main operations abroad. For the founder-abroad track, the question is whether the firm could plausibly have been founded abroad by Danish founders. Candidates that are clearly out of scope, especially firms founded before 1999, are removed, while uncertain but plausible cases are retained. The purpose of this stage is to convert raw leads into a more interpretable and standardized candidate format without becoming prematurely restrictive.

The next stage is a stricter enrichment and reconciliation stage. Here the pipeline moves decisively away from recall and toward conservative evidence organization. The model is asked to verify and refine the candidate record by consulting broader web evidence and reconciling the available information. This includes attempting to identify the earliest legal entity, founding date, founding city and country, current headquarters location, firm status today, and relevant merger or acquisition context. It also includes determining whether the case genuinely fits the requested track or instead appears to belong to the other one.

This stage is essential because the same superficial description can support multiple interpretations. A company described as Danish-founded may in fact have been founded abroad by Danish entrepreneurs. A company described as having established itself in London may simply have opened a foreign office while remaining headquartered in Denmark. A company that later appears under a foreign parent may reflect acquisition and integration rather than an independent relocation of executive functions. The role of the enrichment stage is therefore not merely to collect more facts, but to discipline interpretation and organize the evidence into the most defensible company-level account available.

The final automated stage of the LLM prompting pipeline is a strict validation gate. At this point the question is no longer whether a case is plausible, but whether it satisfies the research definition closely enough to survive into the final review set. A candidate is labeled true only when the relevant criteria are met with sufficiently strong evidence. For the Denmark-founded track, this means evidence that the firm itself was founded, based, or incorporated in Denmark and later moved its own executive headquarters, main office, or main operations abroad. For the founder-abroad track, this means evidence that the firm was founded abroad and that at least one founder appears Danish on the basis of source-backed information. Cases that remain ambiguous, weakly supported, or internally inconsistent are kept as unclear rather than forced into a positive classification. This conservative logic is deliberate, since false positives at this stage would impose unnecessary cost on later human review and would weaken the credibility of the final dataset.

The Diffbot pipeline differs mainly in how it obtains its initial candidates. Instead of generating leads through recall-oriented search prompts, it begins from a third-party CSV derived from Diffbot. In the current project, this source is especially useful for the category of firms founded abroad by Danish founders. The Diffbot records provide a practical starting point because they already contain firm-level hints such as company names, homepage URLs, LinkedIn URLs, founder names, location fields, and summary descriptions. However, the pipeline does not treat these fields as established facts. This is a crucial design choice. Third-party structured databases are useful because they surface candidates efficiently, but they also inherit the limitations of the material they aggregate.

For that reason, the preparation stage of the Diffbot pipeline converts each CSV row into a standardized candidate record while preserving the original values as provenance. The fields are deliberately marked as uncertain at this stage because the purpose is not yet to validate the case, but to make sure that the third-party hint enters the workflow transparently. In effect, the Diffbot pipeline starts later than the LLM prompting pipeline, because it does not need to perform a snowball discovery stage, but it does not start from stronger truth claims. It begins from a different type of clue, not from a more definitive form of evidence.

Once the Diffbot records have been prepared, the downstream logic becomes largely the same as in the LLM prompting pipeline. The candidates are passed through the same enrichment and reconciliation stage, where broader web evidence is used to confirm, reject, or qualify the original hints. The same conceptual distinctions remain central here. A founder-abroad case must not be mistaken for a Denmark-founded relocation case. A foreign office must not be mistaken for a headquarters move. Acquisition-linked changes must not be treated as evidence of an independent relocation unless the focal firm appears to continue with its own headquarters or main operations abroad. The shared downstream logic across the two pipelines is important because it ensures that different entry sources do not lead to different substantive standards.

After enrichment, the Diffbot pipeline applies the same conservative validation stage. The result is again a structured label of true, false, or unclear, together with a short evidence summary and an indication of whether human review is needed. In the current implementation, the Diffbot workflow writes its cumulative outputs into Diffbot-specific files rather than merging them immediately into the general cumulative outputs used elsewhere in the project. This separation is useful because it allows the Diffbot track to be audited independently while preserving comparability with the logic of the other pipelines.

The architecture of the LLM prompting and Diffbot pipelines reflects a classic trade-off between recall and precision. If the workflow were highly restrictive at the beginning, it would likely miss many substantively relevant firms, especially obscure firms or firms whose histories are not stated explicitly. If it remained permissive all the way through, the final review set would become too noisy and too expensive to validate manually. The staged design solves this by keeping the discovery and preparation stages broad, the enrichment stage interpretive but disciplined, and the final validation stage conservative.

Both pipelines culminate in a review-oriented dataset for human close reading. This output preserves the main structured fields relating to founding, relocation, founder identity, current headquarters, merger or acquisition context, confidence, and grouped source URLs. The purpose is not to replace human judgment, but to organize the evidence so that human review becomes more focused, more transparent, and more scalable. Human close reading remains necessary because some of the most substantively interesting cases are also the most ambiguous ones. Firms change names, maintain multiple legal entities, describe themselves differently across sources, or appear in contexts where relocation, founder migration, acquisition, and legal restructuring are difficult to separate cleanly.

Taken together, the LLM prompting and Diffbot pipelines are best understood as complementary identification strategies that feed into the same downstream research process. The LLM prompting pipeline is especially useful for recall expansion beyond the validated seed list, including long-tail cases that may not already appear in curated datasets. The Diffbot pipeline is especially useful when a precompiled founder-oriented list already exists and needs to be validated, cleaned, and classified under the same research rules. In both cases, the methodological principle is the same: preserve plausible candidates early, preserve provenance throughout, apply stricter interpretation only in later stages, and reserve final acceptance for cases that survive conservative validation and human close reading.


Conclusion

Across all three methods, the project relies on a consistent research logic. The Borsen pipeline begins from narrative reporting and attempts to recover relocation-relevant signals from a large article corpus. The LLM prompting pipeline begins from a seed list and searches outward for additional cases through structured web discovery. The Diffbot pipeline begins from a third-party candidate list and subjects those candidates to the same downstream checks. These are different routes into the problem, but they all respond to the same empirical difficulty: the relevant distinction is easy to state conceptually and hard to observe cleanly in raw source material.

The reason the pipeline is structured in stages is therefore not merely technical convenience. It reflects a substantive judgment about how to study a rare and ambiguously described organizational phenomenon. High recall is needed early in order not to miss plausible cases. Stricter evidence reconciliation is needed later in order not to confuse relocation with foreign expansion, founder migration, acquisition, or legal restructuring. Conservative validation and human close reading are needed at the end because the final research categories require interpretive care. The combined pipeline is designed to make those interpretive decisions possible on a scale that would otherwise be difficult to manage, while still preserving the transparency necessary for rigorous research use.
