\section{LLM Prompting and Diffbot Pipelines}

This section describes the two non-article-based identification pipelines used in the project, namely the LLM prompting pipeline and the Diffbot pipeline. The two pipelines differ in their immediate source of candidate firms, but they are designed to serve the same broader research purpose. In both cases, the objective is to identify firms that either were founded in Denmark and later moved their own executive headquarters or main operations abroad, or were founded abroad by Danish founders. The pipelines are therefore best understood as two alternative entry routes into a common validation logic rather than as two fully separate research designs.

The motivation for building these pipelines is straightforward. The underlying phenomenon is difficult to observe directly because firms rarely describe their founding history, headquarters changes, and organizational development in a single clear source. In many cases, the relevant information is fragmented across company websites, startup databases, LinkedIn pages, press releases, archived pages, news articles, and acquisition reports. A pipeline that relies too heavily on any one source risks either missing a large number of relevant cases or accepting many cases that do not in fact meet the research definition. For that reason, both pipelines are designed to preserve relatively broad candidate sets at the beginning and apply stricter reasoning only in later stages.

\subsection{Common Research Logic}

Although the two pipelines begin differently, they follow the same research logic. First, they assemble a set of candidate firms. Second, they preserve the provenance of those candidates rather than immediately collapsing them into a binary classification. Third, they reconcile the candidate record against broader web evidence. Fourth, they apply a conservative validation step that is tied directly to the substantive research definition. Finally, they export the remaining records for human close reading.

This structure is deliberate. The project concerns a distinction that is conceptually important but empirically easy to blur. A firm founded in Denmark that later moved its own executive headquarters abroad is not the same thing as a firm founded abroad by Danish founders. Likewise, a foreign office is not the same thing as a headquarters relocation, a legal redomiciling is not automatically the same thing as a movement of executive functions, and an acquisition is not automatically evidence that the focal firm independently relocated abroad. Because these distinctions are difficult to enforce at the moment of initial discovery, the pipelines are built so that early stages remain recall-oriented while later stages become increasingly conservative.

\subsection{LLM Prompting Pipeline}

The LLM prompting pipeline begins from a validated seed list of already known firms. This seed list matters for two reasons. First, it provides a practical exclusion list so that the discovery stage does not keep returning firms that are already part of the known sample. Second, it provides a concrete set of examples that helps anchor the search process conceptually. In other words, the seed list does not simply tell the pipeline what to exclude; it also helps define what type of firm the discovery process is meant to search for.

The discovery stage itself is intentionally recall-first. The purpose is not to decide at once whether a firm truly belongs in the final research dataset. Instead, the purpose is to find plausible additional firms that may fit the research definition and that can be investigated more carefully later. This is why the search process is organized through bounded discovery buckets, such as industries, destination countries, and combinations of the two. A bucketed search strategy makes it easier to explore the long tail of possible candidates and reduces the tendency of general web search to return only a small set of famous, repeatedly discussed examples. Methodologically, this matters because the project is not only interested in highly visible firms, but also in less prominent firms that may still be substantively relevant.

The output of the discovery stage is a structured set of candidate leads rather than a final list of accepted firms. Each lead records the firm name, the requested origin track, one or more source URLs, and short evidence notes about possible founding location, possible founding year, possible abroad location, and the kind of signal that triggered retrieval. These records are intentionally lightweight. They are designed to preserve why the firm was surfaced and where the hint came from, not to assert that the case has already been verified. This design choice is important because it keeps the discovery stage useful even when the evidence is incomplete, indirect, or uncertain.

After discovery, the pipeline deduplicates the candidate set. This step is necessary because the same firm can appear repeatedly across different sectors, destinations, query formulations, or naming variants. If duplicate records were passed forward unchanged, later stages would be distorted by repeated observations of the same firm and would also become harder to audit. The deduplication stage therefore merges repeated candidates by normalized firm name while preserving the different source URLs and discovery contexts attached to them. In substantive terms, this ensures that candidate frequency is not confused with evidentiary strength.

The next step is a first structured candidate extraction stage. This stage still emphasizes recall, but it already moves beyond raw discovery. At this point the system asks whether the candidate could plausibly fit the relevant research track. For the \texttt{in\_denmark} track, the question is whether the firm could plausibly have been founded in Denmark and later moved its own headquarters, main office, or main operations abroad. For the \texttt{abroad\_danish\_founders} track, the question is whether the firm could plausibly have been founded abroad by Danish founders. This stage allows uncertain but plausible candidates to remain in the sample, while excluding clearly out-of-scope firms, especially firms founded before 1999. The purpose is to transform raw leads into a more interpretable candidate format without becoming prematurely restrictive.

Once the candidates have been structured, the pipeline enters a stricter enrichment and reconciliation stage. This is where the workflow changes most clearly from recall-oriented discovery to source-based evaluation. The model is asked to verify and refine the candidate record by consulting broader web evidence and organizing the available information into a more coherent interpretation. This includes attempting to identify the earliest known legal entity, founding date, founding city and country, current headquarters location, current firm status, and any relevant merger or acquisition context. It also includes deciding whether the evidence really points toward the requested track or instead appears to fit the other track better.

The reason for this stricter enrichment stage is that the same surface description can often support multiple interpretations. A company described as ``Danish-founded'' may in fact have been founded abroad by Danish entrepreneurs. A company that ``expanded to London'' may simply have opened an office there while remaining headquartered in Denmark. A company that later appears under a foreign parent may reflect acquisition and integration rather than an independent relocation of executive functions. The enrichment stage therefore exists not simply to add detail, but to discipline interpretation and reduce the risk that ambiguous cases are coded too quickly.

The final automated stage of the LLM prompting pipeline is a conservative validation gate. At this point the question is no longer whether the case is plausible, but whether it satisfies the research definition closely enough to survive into the final review set. A candidate is labeled \texttt{true} only when the relevant criteria are met with sufficiently strong evidence. For the Denmark-founded track, this requires evidence that the firm itself was founded, based, or incorporated in Denmark and later moved its own executive headquarters, main office, or main operations abroad. For the founder-abroad track, this requires evidence that the firm was founded abroad and that at least one founder appears Danish on the basis of source-backed information. Cases that remain weak, ambiguous, or internally inconsistent are retained as \texttt{unclear} rather than forced into a positive classification. This conservative design is important because false positives at this stage would be costly for later manual review and would also weaken the credibility of the final dataset.

\subsection{Diffbot Pipeline}

The Diffbot pipeline differs from the LLM prompting pipeline mainly in how the candidate set is obtained. Instead of discovering firms through recall-oriented prompting and web search, it begins from a third-party CSV derived from Diffbot. In the current project, this source is especially useful for the track concerning firms founded abroad by Danish founders. The Diffbot list provides a practical starting point because it already contains firm-level hints such as company names, homepage URLs, LinkedIn URLs, founder names, location fields, and summary descriptions.

However, the pipeline does not treat Diffbot as a source of established facts. This is a crucial methodological choice. Third-party structured databases are useful because they can surface candidates efficiently, but they also inherit errors, omissions, and simplifications from the web material they aggregate. For that reason, the Diffbot fields are treated as hints rather than conclusions. The preparation stage converts each CSV row into a standardized candidate record while preserving the original values as source provenance. The fields are deliberately marked as uncertain at this point because the purpose of the step is not to validate the case, but to make sure that the third-party clue enters the workflow in a transparent and auditable form.

This design reflects a broader principle that runs through the project. A good candidate source should not be confused with a final evidentiary source. Diffbot is useful because it can point toward relevant firms quickly, especially in cases where founder identity and cross-border location are part of the story. But those hints still need to be checked against broader evidence. By keeping the preparation stage distinct from the later validation stages, the pipeline preserves the efficiency advantage of Diffbot without giving the database more epistemic authority than it deserves.

After the initial preparation step, the downstream logic of the Diffbot pipeline becomes largely the same as in the LLM prompting pipeline. The candidate records are passed into the stricter enrichment and reconciliation stage, where the system uses broader web evidence to check the original hints. The same conceptual distinctions apply. A founder-abroad case must not be mistaken for a Denmark-founded relocation case. A foreign office must not be mistaken for a headquarters move. Acquisition-linked changes must not be treated as evidence of an independent headquarters relocation unless the focal firm appears to continue with its own headquarters or main operations abroad. The enrichment stage thus serves the same purpose here as in the LLM pipeline: it converts raw candidate hints into a more defensible company-level interpretation.

The Diffbot records are then passed through the same conservative validation stage. Again, the aim is not to maximize the number of accepted firms, but to ensure that the surviving records are aligned with the substantive research definition. In the current implementation, the Diffbot workflow writes its cumulative outputs into separate Diffbot-specific files. This is useful from a research management perspective because it allows the Diffbot track to be audited independently and compared with other identification routes without losing the fact that all tracks share the same underlying logic of evaluation.

\subsection{Why the Two Pipelines Are Structured This Way}

The architecture of these pipelines reflects a trade-off between recall and precision that is common in research settings where the relevant phenomenon is rare, ambiguously described, and distributed across many sources. If the workflow were highly restrictive at the beginning, it would likely miss many substantively relevant firms, especially obscure firms and firms whose relocation history is not stated explicitly. If, by contrast, the workflow remained permissive all the way through, the later review set would become too noisy and too costly to evaluate manually. The staged design attempts to solve this problem by keeping early discovery broad and later validation narrow.

This design also reflects the fact that the final research question is not simply whether a firm has some connection to Denmark and some connection to a foreign country. The relevant question is more specific: whether the firm belongs to one of two carefully distinguished categories. Because those categories are easy to conflate in raw web material, the pipeline must do more than retrieve names. It must preserve enough structured evidence to support later distinctions between founding origin, founder identity, headquarters relocation, foreign expansion, acquisition, and legal restructuring. In this sense, the pipeline is not just a search tool, but a mechanism for disciplined evidence organization.

\subsection{Final Output and Role of Human Review}

The final output of both pipelines is a review-oriented dataset for human close reading. This output preserves the key structured fields relating to founding, relocation, founder identity, current headquarters, merger or acquisition context, confidence, and grouped source URLs. The purpose is to hand over to the human reviewer a candidate set that is already substantially organized, but not artificially over-resolved. Human review remains necessary because some of the most important cases are also the most ambiguous ones. Firms can change names, maintain multiple legal entities, describe themselves differently across sources, or appear in contexts where relocation, acquisition, and founder migration are difficult to separate cleanly.

For that reason, the pipelines should not be seen as substitutes for close reading. Their role is to make close reading more focused, more transparent, and more scalable. They reduce the search space, preserve provenance, and apply consistent intermediate logic, but they do not eliminate the need for final judgment. In the context of this project, that is a strength rather than a weakness. The purpose of the automated stages is not to automate away ambiguity, but to organize it in a way that makes rigorous final classification possible.

\subsection{Summary}

Taken together, the LLM prompting pipeline and the Diffbot pipeline are best understood as complementary identification strategies that feed into the same downstream research process. The LLM prompting pipeline is especially useful for recall expansion because it can search broadly for additional firms beyond the validated seed list, including long-tail cases that may not already appear in curated databases. The Diffbot pipeline is especially useful when a precompiled founder-oriented candidate list already exists and needs to be validated, cleaned, and classified under the same research rules. In both cases, the central methodological principle is the same: preserve plausible candidates early, preserve provenance throughout, apply stricter interpretation only in later stages, and reserve final acceptance for cases that survive conservative validation and human close reading.
